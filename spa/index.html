Overview · v0.4 draft · May 2026
Sovereign Personal Agent · Substrate / Lens / Frame Protocol

The grant-semantics layer
for individual sovereignty.

What individuals own, what regulators audit, what agents are allowed to do on their behalf, composed into one protocol primitive. People own, manage, and control their data across organizations and over time; agents act for them, not in place of them.

For the non-developer

Your data, your keys, and the agents that act on your behalf live in three different worlds today, in your phone, in a vendor's database, and in a chat window that forgets you. SLF is the protocol that lets all three speak the same language while keeping you in charge of every grant of authority.

For the developer / privacy professional

Substrate / Lens / Frame is a unified operational primitive: signed facts with regulatory metadata baked in, role-and-jurisdiction projections that respect substrate gates by construction, and outcome-bound frames that emit signed receipts as first-class output. It composes with FIDO AATWG, AP2, Microsoft Entra Agent ID, ERC-8004, MCP, and OID4VP, it doesn't replace any of them.

The problem nobody is solving

SLF · 01 / Problem

Two different audiences arrive at the same gap from opposite ends.

Organizations, healthcare systems, financial services, government agencies, any institution that has to honor jurisdiction-specific regulations, need agents that can operate across organizational boundaries. The pieces exist. Regulatory metadata lives in policy stores (Microsoft Purview, AWS Macie). Capabilities live in OAuth scopes and capability tokens. Audit trails live siloed inside each system that touched the data. What does not exist is a way to make those three layers travel together through a single agent transaction, so that an agent acting across a pharmacy, a payer, and a provider in the same workflow carries the right regulatory tags, the right grants, and emits a verifiable audit receipt at every hop. The EU AI Act Article 50 transparency obligations come into force in December 2026 under the Digital Omnibus, with Annex III high-risk obligations phasing through 2027 and beyond. Regulators describe outcomes, not protocols, but the current stack, where regulatory metadata sits apart from the data and audit trails are stitched together after the fact, struggles to demonstrate the per-action provenance these obligations imply.

Individuals arrive from the opposite end. Today's "personal AI", the ChatGPTs and the Rewinds and the Personal .ais, gives you an agent runtime, but it owns your keys, it owns your data, and it owns the model that interprets them. Switch providers and you start over. Solid and similar projects built a sovereign substrate but never built the agent. Prior attempts entangled the two primitives. The result, predictably, is corporate ownership of the layer that should be most yours.

Both ends of the problem need the same bridge: an identity-and-data substrate the individual or institution owns, plus an agent runtime that is fully replaceable, with a protocol between them that carries regulatory semantics, capability grants, and audit receipts as first-class objects. SLF is that bridge.

Substrate, lens, frame

SLF · 02 / Primitives

Every operation in the protocol, issuing a credential, granting an agent permission to spend, revoking a previous grant, executing an action, recording an audit trail, is encoded as the same three-part object. Every operation encodes the same three-part object. One shape recurs at every layer the protocol touches.

view = render(substrate, lens, frame) → receipt

Read it as: a view onto your data is always a substrate projected through a lens, bound to a frame, and accompanied by a signed receipt.

Substrate
The thing being operated on
A signed fact. A verifiable credential. A grant. A receipt of a prior operation. Every substrate carries its own type, its issuer, its timestamps, and its intrinsic gates, the regulatory metadata that travels with the data, not stored in a separate policy system. HIPAA scope, GDPR purpose limitation, audience restriction, expiry, no-further-disclosure: all signed metadata, all riding the substrate.
Lens
The projection that narrows
A lens binds a role and a jurisdiction at a moment. A pharmacist's lens reads protected health information; a payer's lens reads claims facts; a personal lens reads everything you own. The lens filters what is visible, but lenses respect substrate gates by construction. A lens cannot bypass a gate; it can only narrow the field of what the gate already allows.
Frame
The bound action
A frame names the actor, the intended outcome, the approvals required, and the audit context. Grants authorize specific frames, not open-ended access. Human-in-the-loop approvals happen at frame boundaries. When the frame closes, it produces a signed Receipt SLF, the audited result is the first-class output, not a side-channel log.

This is the architectural payoff: credential issuance, presentation, grant revocation, agent action, audit emission, cross-organizational compound workflow, all of them are SLFs. One primitive, learned once, that works everywhere the protocol touches.

How it composes with everything else

SLF · 03 / Composition

Eight emerging protocols are converging on the agent-authorization slot, FIDO's Agentic Authentication TWG (chartered April 2026), Google's AP2 mandates, OpenAI's Agentic Commerce Protocol, Microsoft Entra Agent ID, ERC-8004 on Ethereum, Apple Wallet's App Intents bridge, Visa's TAP, Stripe's MPP. All of them are answering a different question than SLF.

They define agent identity (who is this agent?) and authorization mechanics (does this token grant this scope?). Critical work. None of them define what the grants mean at the policy layer, substrate-bound regulatory tags that travel with credentials, an action-typed grant taxonomy that distinguishes read from read-and-cache from copy from write-back from sync from action from compound, or Substrate → Lens → Frame composition with deterministic narrowing for cross-jurisdictional flows. That is the layer SLF occupies.

Grant semantics substrate-bound · action-typed · composable
Delegation & intent who delegated, what intent
Agent identity & token mechanics who is the agent, what scopes
Substrate transport & credential format how facts travel between parties

Read it as: four conceptual layers. SLF occupies only the top one. Multiple in-flight protocols sit at each of the lower three, the named candidates change quarter to quarter, and SLF is designed to compose with whichever set wins at each layer.

At the substrate-transport layer, the obvious candidates are OID4VP, mdoc, and W3C Verifiable Credentials data models. At agent identity and token mechanics, Microsoft Entra Agent ID and ERC-8004 are the institutional and on-chain options, with several OAuth Working Group Internet Drafts, DAAP, AAT, ACAP, actor-chain, consolidating the agent-token mechanics in parallel. At delegation and intent, FIDO's Agentic Authentication Technical Working Group is the standards forum, and Google's AP2 mandates plus OpenAI's Agentic Commerce Protocol are the early movers in the payment-bounded slice.

The strategic posture is cohabitation. SLF doesn't pick winners at the lower layers; it specifies the substrate-bound regulatory semantics, action-typed grant taxonomy, and substrate → lens → frame composition that compose above whichever set is in play. Fighting any of the converging stacks loses. Layering above them is the lane, and as those stacks consolidate, SLF's surface area gets clearer rather than threatened.

Scope and approach

SLF · 04 / Scope

Three artifacts are in flight in parallel: the SLF v0.4 architecture; a Phase 1 prototype (the SPA Recovery Prototype) advancing as a Cargo workspace; and a layered OAuth Working Group extension drafted as draft-crenshaw-oauth-slf-substrate-grants-00, which specifies how the SLF primitive composes above existing agent-token-mechanics drafts at the IETF. The proposed direction is OAuth WG submission once the composition sections settle; the v0.4 spec deliverable is deliberately narrow, enough of the protocol to demonstrate the architectural payoff end to end, leaving production hardening and platform integration to a Phase 2 decision.

In scope · SLF v0.4
  • Decentralized identifiers with hardware-backed key custody
  • Substrate-bound intrinsic gates (regulatory metadata travels with facts)
  • Seven-type grant taxonomy: read, read-and-cache, copy, write-back, sync, action, compound
  • Substrate → Lens → Frame composition with deterministic narrowing
  • Receipt SLFs as first-class output of every operation
  • Recovery primitives: FROST 2-of-3 threshold signing, receipt-chain verification
Out of scope · deferred to Phase 2+
  • iOS Secure Enclave integration (Phase 2)
  • Real cloud backends for Apple / Google ID transport (Phase 2)
  • Zero-knowledge proofs of backup correctness (Phase 2–3)
  • UX research with non-cryptographer users (Phase 2)
  • Enterprise lens-pack productization (option value, 5 years+)

The layered OAuth extension makes a second kind of scope statement explicit: what SLF deliberately does not specify. Agent-identifier formats, delegation-chain construction, scope-subsumption algorithms, cascade-revocation mechanisms, and audit-chain constructions for token issuance and use all belong to the agent-token-mechanics layer the extension composes above. Concretely, that means SLF defers to drafts like DAAP (persistent agent identifiers, grant/revocation/audit), AAT (capability tokens with typed-constraint subsumption), ACAP (cryptographic intent binding), and actor-chain (cross-domain token-exchange preservation under RFC 8693). Sections 5 through 8 of the SLF draft work out the composition with each. Cohabitation moves from strategic posture into a normative specification.

Sequencing is deliberate. The Phase 1 prototype proves the architecture end to end at the CLI; the OAuth WG draft makes the composition surface concrete and citable. How the operational ecosystem around the protocol, conformance suite, vocabulary registries, reference implementations, gets stewarded is itself an open question; foundation incorporation is one possible path, not the only one, and probably not the first move unless funding circumstances require it.

FIDO's Agentic Authentication Technical Working Group is the one slot where SLF must engage substantively. Strategic opportunities

SLF · 06 / Opportunities

Three forcing functions converge over the next twelve months. Each one is a clock; together they describe why the Foundation incorporation work is on the critical path next to the prototype.

The regulated-domain anchor

In March 2026, CVS Health announced a cross-organization agentic AI platform (Google Cloud and Gemini) orchestrating pharmacy, payer, PBM, and provider workflows. That is the regulated-domain cross-org grant problem SLF was designed for, named publicly by a Fortune 5 company. Healthcare systems forced to run agents across pharmacy, payer, and provider boundaries are the clearest early adopters of substrate-bound regulatory semantics.

The regulatory ramp

The window is broader than a single cliff. EU AI Act Article 50 transparency obligations now come into force 2 December 2026 under the Digital Omnibus provisional agreement (7 May 2026); Annex III high-risk obligations slip roughly sixteen months and phase in through 2027 and beyond. Across the same window, India's DPDP consent-manager mandate (November 2026), the EU Digital Product Passport for batteries (February 2027), and CMS-0057-F in the United States all push the same direction: per-action provenance, demonstrable consent, structured audit. SLF is not what any of these statutes requires, regulators describe outcomes, and industry picks standards. But the architectural shape (substrate-bound regulatory metadata, signed receipts as first-class output) is well-formed for institutions whose compliance posture has to scale across organizations, and the 2027–2030 ramp is enough runway to do that work deliberately rather than reactively.

Where a moat could come from

Specs get absorbed by standards bodies; ideas spread. What standards bodies cannot absorb is operational infrastructure, federated vocabulary registries for gates and reason codes, a paid conformance suite, reference implementations across the major language ecosystems, and a cross-protocol composition layer no standards body owns. If SLF develops a defensible position, it is more likely to be operational than legal.

Whether that operational layer should be carried by a standalone SLF Foundation is genuinely open. The current planning is to defer foundation incorporation unless a funding offer materializes that requires it. A 501(c)(3) sister entity to Lexenne is one option; an SLF project inside an established neutral host (LF Decentralized Trust, OpenWallet Foundation) is another; publishing through a W3C Community Group or IETF Internet Draft is a third; and deferring all of it until Patina ships to a paying customer is a fourth, with its own credibility logic, standards work that follows deployed product usually lands harder than the reverse. The governance deep-dive works through the five named risks, the four legitimate alternatives, and the honest probability distribution.
